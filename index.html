    Eleven hundred hours.

    That is how long one Heure Bleue takes, from a bar of gold to a watch on a wrist. These are the hands that spend them.

    First

    The dial is turned.

    Florian runs the 1902 rose engine. He cuts each line of the sunburst in one pass, then the dial is fired in blue lacquer seven times until the guilloché shows through like water.

    16hours7coats of lacquer

    Second

    The bridges are engraved.

    Mireille cuts arabesques into the gold bridges freehand. She works under a microscope, so the curls of gold coming off the burin are thinner than a hair.

    40hours9bridges

    Third

    Every part is finished.

    Geneva stripes on the plates, perlage under the dial, black polish on the screw heads. The bevels on each steel part are filed and polished by hand until they catch light like a mirror.

    420hours312parts

    Last

    One watchmaker builds it.

    Assembly, regulation and casing are done by one person, who signs the inside of the caseback. If the watch ever comes back for service, it goes to the same bench.

    14days31days of testing

    Four generations, one valley.

    The Vallée de Joux sits a thousand metres up in the Jura. Winters are long, so farmers made watch parts by the window. We never left.

    1. 1884

      A workbench by the window

      Élise Vaucret, a farmer's daughter, begins finishing movements for the large houses of Geneva from her kitchen in Le Brassus.

    2. 1902

      The rose engine arrives

      Her son buys a guilloché machine from a closing workshop in Paris. It still cuts every Vaucret dial.

    3. 1969

      We said no to quartz

      When the industry moved to battery watches, the house kept its four watchmakers and made fewer watches instead.

    4. 2004

      Our own movement

      Calibre V21 is designed in the valley, the first movement made entirely under the Vaucret name.

    5. Today

      Eleven watches a year

      Nine watchmakers, one rose engine and a waiting list. Élise's great-granddaughter, also Élise, runs the bench.
